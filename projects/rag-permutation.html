Is it the Pruning, or the Ordering?

The confound

When a retrieval-augmented system answers a question, something fetches ten passages and pastes them into the prompt. Long prompts are slow and expensive, so there is now a whole category of tools that read those ten and throw most of them away, keeping the two or three that matter. Every published evaluation scores them the same way: feed the passages in, check the answer is still right.

Two things were already known. Language models care about the order things appear in their prompt. And pruning tools throw passages away. Those two facts interact. When you delete passages 3, 5 and 7 from a list of ten, you do not only remove them, you promote everything underneath. Passage 8 slides up into a more prominent slot.

Stated precisely: a context pruning method is scored against a reference point that moves when the method acts. Reported gains confound evidence selection with positional promotion, and a single-order evaluation cannot separate them. This is not a new pruning method. It is an evaluation protocol, two controls, and a set of numbers.

The two controls

The first is shuffling. Ask the same question five times over, with the surviving passages in a different order each time. Same words, same content, greedy decoding everywhere so that sampling noise cannot be mistaken for ordering noise. That isolates the position effect.

The second does the real work. placebo_pos is a pruner that discards exactly as many passages as a real method, chosen by position alone, without reading a word. It is the equivalent of testing someone who claims they can pick the three best bottles out of ten by taste against a person who grabs bottles 1, 9 and 10 and never opens any of them. If a method cannot beat that at equal keep-count, it is not selecting on content.

What came out

Reordering an identical context changes the answer about half the time; only 35% of questions return a byte-identical answer under all five orderings

Pruning methods beat the placebo by +0.2760 token-F1 — the main hypothesis was that they exploit position, and the data did not support it

But no practical method separates from plain rerank-and-truncate by more than the noise that reordering alone creates

Ask an LLM which passages to keep, reorder them, ask again: it picks differently in 98 of 100 questions

Order changes the answer about half the time

Same passages, same words, greedy decoding, only the order differs. Half of questions swing by about 0.39 token-F1, on a metric bounded at 0 and 1. There is no "typical" question: the distribution has two modes and little in between. And "unchanged" needs care, because on the other half the score does not move while the model still gives a different answer on 15% of all questions, wrong in a different way each time and scoring zero either way.

It is not a formatting artifact. Re-running the whole thing with the context fenced in <context> tags instead of bare moves the effect by 0.0047 token-F1, 95% CI [−0.0140, 0.0239], which does not separate from zero. That check was registered before the main run.

What drives it is the number of slots, not only the evidence. Holding the retained gold passages fixed and varying only how many passages the context presents, the swing still rises by +0.0372 to +0.0809 token-F1 going from 2 slots to 5 or 10, in every evidence stratum. It holds even when the context contains neither gold passage. Most of the sensitivity is bought by the first few slots and it flattens after five, so pruning hard reduces order sensitivity as a side effect.

The pruners are selecting on content

This was the study's main hypothesis and the data did not support it. Real pruners beat the placebo by +0.2760 token-F1, 95% CI [0.2223, 0.3297], at every budget tested. The control behaves too: an arm that drops passages at random is indistinguishable from the placebo, exactly as it should be.

Yet method choice matters less than a formatting decision

Measure each method's gain in units of "how much does the score move when you just reshuffle the passages", and every practical method fails to separate from simple rerank-and-truncate. The one arm that clears that bar is a cheating upper bound that peeks at the answer. Put carefully: in this setting, the spread between pruning methods is smaller than the spread a single method shows across orderings of the same passages.

Pruning is still worth doing. Against keeping all ten passages, the best pruner reduces the context to 27% with no statistically distinguishable loss in token-F1 (−0.011, [−0.0510, +0.0284]), while a plain rerank-and-truncate loses 0.045 and does separate from zero. Read narrowly: "cost" here means answer quality under token-F1 on this dataset and generator. Latency, compute, and the pruner's own selection overhead were not measured anywhere in this study.

The strange one: methods that are order-dependent inside themselves

The same effect, one level further in. Ask a language model which three passages to keep, then show it the same three in a different order and ask again. Agreement between its three selections is 0.213, where 1.000 would mean order makes no difference and 0.047 is random guessing. The selection changed in 98 of 100 questions, and in 23 of them the three attempts had no passage in common at all. The tool does not really have an answer. It has a distribution over answers, and a single run gives you one draw from it.

LLMLingua-2 compresses a concatenated context differently depending on the order it is given: 0 of 100 passages survive identically across orderings when applied the normal way. Unlike the LLM pruner this is expected rather than anomalous, since reordering genuinely changes its input. What is worth knowing is the scale, because people treat the compressed output as a property of the passage set, and not one passage in a hundred survives that assumption.

The LLM pruner also fails to name the requested number of passages in 24.3% of cases, and that rate reproduces to four decimal places on a 27B model from a different size class (0.2432 against 0.2433). It is a property of asking a model to name k items, not a quirk of one small model.

Does it survive scale?

A hosted replication on a 27B model was matched to the main run exactly: same questions, same three orderings, byte-identical passage orders, so only the generator differs. Order sensitivity is intact, with every interval excluding zero at the primary budget. But on an un-pruned context the 3B's swing is 4.5× the 27B's (0.1668 against 0.0374, paired difference 0.1294 [0.0679, 0.1910]). The 27B still answers 16% of questions differently on order alone.

So the protocol transfers and the magnitudes do not. Every number above should be read as belonging to a 3B rather than to generators in general. The placebo gap replicates at 27B; the LLM pruner's budget defect is scale-invariant where the ordering effect is not.

Memorization is not the explanation. Only 8.7% of questions can be answered exactly with no passages at all, and the analysis is restricted to questions the model gets wrong without context, where the no-context score is 0.0089 token-F1. That rate triples with scale, to 26% on the 27B, which is why the filter is recomputed per generator rather than shared.

The part I care most about

Before collecting any data I wrote down what I expected, what would count as success, and exactly which comparisons I would run, then committed that to version control so the timestamps prove it. The registered text is retrievable at a commit dated two days before the first main-run result. That is what stops you quietly redefining success once you have seen how things turned out — and it is what makes the headline finding here reportable as a failed hypothesis rather than quietly reframed.

Next to it is a log of every mistake found afterwards, published in full: a caching bug that quietly corrupted one method's results from the day it was written, a statistic that reported a rounding error as a real finding, a check promised in advance and then never run. None of them changed a conclusion. They are all in there anyway, because a log that records only the things that went right would not be much of a log.

255 tests pass in about 15 seconds. Every generation is cached on a hash of the model, prompt and decode parameters and flushed as it goes, so reruns are free and interrupting a long run loses nothing.

Limitations

Pruner checkpoints are used as published on a dataset they were not tuned for, so this measures deployed behaviour rather than each method's ceiling. Results come from one dataset and one model family, which is the main limitation. A cross-family probe confirms the effect exists elsewhere but not its size, and the 27B replication within the same lineage shows it shrinking about fourfold with scale — on two points, which cannot tell a smooth decay from a threshold. The reference ordering is the dataset's own as-given paragraph order rather than a retriever ranking, since the distractor setting has no retriever.
