Attention Sinks and Quantization: Does the Architectural Fix Still Pay?

The question

Large language models concentrate a disproportionate share of attention mass on a handful of token positions, usually position 0. Those positions carry extreme residual-stream outliers. Both facts are established, and neither is re-tested here. The claim under audit is the third one: that these outliers are what breaks low-bit activation quantization, and that mitigating them architecturally buys measurable robustness.

That literature was largely built against per-tensor activation quantization and pre-QK-Norm architectures. Both have moved on. The question is whether the architectural fix still pays once it is compared against a properly configured modern baseline, meaning per-token activation scaling on models that already use QK-Norm, and reported with confidence intervals, which almost none of the prior work does.

A negative result, that the architectural fix is largely redundant under modern quantization practice, was treated as the null hypothesis rather than a disappointment. The result came back partly negative, which turned out to be the more interesting outcome.

Setup

Five checkpoints, three of which are 1.7B models from the same training run that differ by a single boolean in config.json: an ungated baseline, a head-wise gate adding 0.1% parameters, and an element-wise gate adding 12%. The gated checkpoints are Qiu et al. (NeurIPS 2025), used as released rather than reimplemented. GPT-2 small and Qwen3-0.6B-Base supply cross-architecture context.

The headline metric is D_sink = Δppl(all quantized) − Δppl(sink tokens fp16): the quantization damage attributable specifically to the sink positions, in nats, with 95% sequence-bootstrap confidence intervals over 32 held-out sequences. 360 grid cells on 281 FineWeb-Edu documents at 8, 6 and 4-bit activations, plus a 60-cell replication on a second corpus of Python source, and one downstream task.

The fix works: on the matched pair, per-tensor sink damage falls 55× (+0.6450 → +0.0117 nats) for 0.1% extra parameters

It is redundant: switching the quantizer from per-tensor to per-token scaling buys the same 32–46× reduction on every model, with no architecture change

The standard metric ranks the worst model first: the variant with the lowest sink mass is the only checkpoint per-tensor 8-bit quantization destroys

Each result on its own gives a misleading impression. The first says the fix works. The second says it is unnecessary. The third says the best-known variant of it is harmful in exactly the setting it was designed for. A paper reporting only the first column would be correct but misleading; one reporting only the second would conclude the mitigation does not work.

Sink mass is not a sufficient predictor

Ranking the checkpoints by mean sink mass and then by how much damage per-tensor 8-bit activation quantization actually does puts them in nearly opposite orders at the top:

The element-wise gate carries the least sink mass in the set and takes 195× more damage than the ungated baseline it is meant to improve on, against an unquantized perplexity of 14.5. Selecting or evaluating an architecture on sink mass alone, as much of this literature does, uses a proxy that can rank two real models the wrong way round, and it does so here on the pair a practitioner would most likely be choosing between.

Attention entropy explains the direction. Head-wise holds entropy nearly flat across depth (3.04 → 2.76), while element-wise still concentrates sharply (2.96 → 1.53), closer to the ungated baseline's fall of 1.26 than to its head-wise sibling. So "sink-free" does not imply "unconcentrated", and the two gates are not doing the same thing.

The claim made is narrow: this is an existence proof that the standard metric inverts on real checkpoints. The stronger claim, that sink mass is the wrong metric in general, is not supported by two arms of one lab's training run and is not made.

Where the damage actually is

The obvious suspect was the gate itself, fused into q_proj as extra output width. Leaving q_proj entirely in fp16, gate included, changes nothing: the model is destroyed either way. Exempting o_proj, which consumes the gated output, recovers about 10%, which on a model at 235× its reference perplexity is not a rescue. The amplification explanation was tested and is wrong.

Per-tensor and per-token scaling differ in exactly one respect, which is whether the scale is shared across token rows. Same rounding, same clamp, same bit width. So the entire gap must be expressible as a property of how magnitude is distributed across rows. Measuring that directly on every layer's input, while quantizing nothing, localised the failure to one tensor: the layer-0 MLP input feeding both branches of the SwiGLU.

Under a shared 8-bit scale that tensor loses 99.26% of its entries to rounding, against 9.6% for the ungated baseline. Under per-row scales the same tensor loses 46%, elevated but survivable, which is why per-token damage stays at +0.68 nats on the same checkpoint. Holding that single MLP in fp16, three modules out of 196, takes the model from 241× its reference perplexity back to 1.45×.

That failure is not sink-attributable at all: holding the sink in fp16 does not rescue it, and the element-wise checkpoint has no residual outlier channel to exempt in the first place. Whatever destroys it, a residual-stream outlier is not it.

Does any of it reach behaviour?

Everything above is an ordering of perplexity damage, and this project's corrections log is mostly about perplexity orderings that did not survive a second corpus or a second threshold. LAMBADA, scored as greedy exact-match on 1000 examples, was the independent check planned from the start. The protocol reproduces a published number first: GPT-2 small scores 0.3070 against roughly 0.326 published on the full 5153 examples.

The fix is worth 25 accuracy points on the matched pair under per-tensor: the ungated baseline loses 35.1 points, the head-wise gate loses 9.7, with non-overlapping intervals, for one boolean and 0.1% extra parameters. But perplexity overstates the size of that win. Total Δppl makes head-wise a 7.8× improvement (+17.94 → +2.29); accuracy makes it 3.6× (0.351 → 0.097). Same direction, less than half the magnitude, which is the kind of calibration a second metric exists to provide.

The damage also concentrates on the hard tokens. LAMBADA target perplexity goes to 7.11× reference on the baseline under per-tensor, against a 2.22× whole-corpus ratio on FineWeb-Edu. Quantization hurts most on exactly the tokens that require long-range context, which an averaged perplexity hides.

Guarding the metric

D_sink is a difference between two damaged models. Once both are destroyed, that difference no longer says anything about which degrades more gracefully. Any cell whose quantized perplexity exceeds 10× its own reference is flagged as destroyed in code, hatched in the figures, and excluded from ratio columns. The threshold is a judgement call, so the sweep is published: measured ratios cluster at 1.01–2.22×, two borderline cells at 8.7× and 8.9×, and 15 cells at 42.7× and above. A threshold of 10× falls inside the empty gap, and anything from 10× to 20× behaves identically.

An earlier version of this work made the sink-mass argument from D_sink itself, which was invalid for exactly this reason: at Δppl +3600 the element-wise per-tensor cell is a destroyed model. The claim survived the correction in a stronger form, based on total damage rather than a difference of differences, and the flagging is now enforced in code rather than left to the reader. That correction, and 27 others, are in the repository's corrections log.

Limitations

The largest model here is 1.7B, so none of this is frontier-scale. The controlled set is one lab's training run, which makes the metric-inversion result an existence proof rather than a population study. The element-wise arm confounds gating with a 12% capacity increase and is not parameter-matched. Quantization is simulated: no int8 kernels, no speedup measured, so nothing here says anything about latency. LAMBADA is one task, in one language, at one sample size.

A pretraining arm for softmax1 was specified, partly built, and deliberately cut. softmax1 cannot be retrofitted onto pretrained weights because the output is the original convex combination scaled down uniformly, so a retrofitted model would degrade for reasons that say nothing about the mechanism. The identity is verified in tests; the intervention remains unevaluated with confidence intervals, here and elsewhere.
